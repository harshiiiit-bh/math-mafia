⚜️ French Revolution & the Idea of the Nation

  • 1789 sovereignty shifted from monarchy to citizens — birth of the "nation" as a political idea.
  • La patrie (fatherland) & le citoyen (citizen); tricolour flag; National Assembly; uniform laws & weights/measures; Paris-dialect French made the national language.
  • Revolutionaries believed France's mission was to liberate Europe from despotism → Jacobin clubs spread; French armies (1790s) carried nationalism into Holland, Belgium, Switzerland, Italy.
  • Napoleon's Civil Code (1804): equality before law, end of birth privileges, right to property — but also heavy taxation, censorship, forced conscription → local resentment grew.

🧩 A Patchwork Continent

  • Mid-18th century: no "nation-states." Germany, Italy, Switzerland = scattered kingdoms/duchies/cantons.
  • Habsburg Empire — a patchwork of German, Italian, Magyar, Polish peoples held together only by loyalty to the emperor, not a shared identity.
  • The new middle class, born with industrialisation (England from late 18th c.; France/Germany in the 19th c.), became the chief carrier of nationalist ideas.

LIBERAL NATIONALISM STOOD FOR

  • Individual freedom, equality before law, government by consent
  • Free markets — e.g. Zollverein (1834), Prussian-led customs union

BUT NOT FOR

  • Universal suffrage — vote mostly limited to property-owning men
  • Women's or the poor's political rights

🔒 Conservatism After 1815

  • 1815 Congress of Vienna, hosted by Austrian Chancellor Metternich — restored Bourbon monarchy in France, redrew borders to contain future French expansion, restored conservative monarchies, imposed censorship.
  • Secret societies of revolutionaries pushed back: Giuseppe Mazzini founded Young Italy & Young Europe, believing nations were the natural units of mankind.

🎭 Age of Revolutions, 1830–1848

  • 1830 July Revolution (France) — Bourbons overthrown, Louis Philippe installed; sparks Belgium's break from the Netherlands.
  • 1821 Greek War of Independence against the Ottomans — European poets & artists (Lord Byron) rallied support; Treaty of Constantinople (1832) recognised Greece.
  • Romanticism: Herder & the Grimm Brothers championed das volk (folk culture) as the true source of national spirit, over cold reason.
  • Silesian weavers' revolt (1845) against exploitative contractors, amid unemployment and competition from cheap English goods.
  • 1848 Paris revolution topples Louis Philippe; parallel liberal Frankfurt Parliament drafts a constitution for a united Germany — rejected by the Prussian king, movement crushed, though monarchs later loosened serfdom in Habsburg lands & Russia.

🤝 The Pact Between Nations — Sorrieu's Utopia

  • French artist Frédéric Sorrieu made a series of four prints in 1848 imagining his dream of a world remade into "democratic and social Republics."
  • This first print shows the peoples of Europe and America — France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Hungary, and beyond — marching in a long procession, each nation identified by its flag and national costume, to pay homage before the statue of Liberty.
  • Leading the march is France, torch of liberty in hand, followed arm-in-arm by other nations as equal, fraternal partners — a visual "pact" of solidarity, not conquest.
  • Above the procession, Christ, saints and angels look on, blessing the pact — linking the new idea of "the nation" to older, familiar religious imagery so ordinary people could grasp it.
  • Sorrieu's vision was utopian: it imagined nations founded on fraternity, choosing unity over war — though real unification (Germany, Italy) came instead through diplomacy and conflict, not a peaceful pact.

🇩🇪 Unifying Germany & Italy

GERMANY

  • Prussia led unification via army & bureaucracy under Otto von Bismarck's "Blood and Iron" policy
  • Three wars (Austria, Denmark, France), 1866–71 → Jan 1871, King William I proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles

ITALY

  • Split among Austria, the Pope, Bourbon Spain & Sardinia-Piedmont
  • Chief Minister Cavour led diplomacy; Garibaldi's "Red Shirts" took South Italy (1860)
  • 1861 — Victor Emmanuel II crowned king of united Italy

👑 Britain — A Different Path

  • No sudden revolution — a gradual process. Parliament, dominant after 1688, forged a "British nation": Act of Union with Scotland (1707); Ireland forcibly incorporated (1801).
  • Scottish & Irish culture actively suppressed; English symbols (Union Jack, "God Save the King") promoted as national symbols for the whole kingdom.

🗽 Visualising the Nation

  • Nations personified as women — abstract ideas made visible as allegories.
  • France → Marianne (red liberty cap, tricolour, cockade); Germany → Germania (oak-leaf crown for heroism, sword, broken chains for freedom).
💥

Turning point: after 1871, nationalism curdled into an aggressive, narrow creed. The Balkans — a Slav region under a crumbling Ottoman Empire, fought over by Russia, Germany, Britain & Austria-Hungary — became the powder keg that triggered the First World War (1914). Later, colonised nations borrowed the "nation-state" idea for their own freedom struggles.

📖 Language, Culture & Resistance

  • Poland, partitioned among Russia, Prussia & Austria, had no independent existence — yet Polish language was made a tool of national resistance against Russian rule.
  • Russian replaced Polish in schools from 1831; clergy & students defied this in secret classes — language became a symbol of the fight to keep a national identity alive.
  • Composers and painters, like Frédéric Chopin, wove folk dances into their music, smuggling nationalist feeling past the censor.

🕊️ WWI, Khilafat & Satyagraha

  • WWI sent defence spending & taxes soaring, prices doubled (1913–18), forced recruitment; 1918–21 crop failures + influenza epidemic killed 12–13 million.
  • Satyagraha (Gandhi): the power of truth & non-violence to persuade — not force — the oppressor. Proven in Champaran (indigo peasants, 1917), Ahmedabad mill workers & Kheda peasants (1918).
  • Rowlatt Act (1919) allowed detention without trial. Gandhi's hartal (6 April) met with repression → Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919, General Dyer) — hundreds killed.
  • Khilafat issue: Ottoman Turkey's defeat threatened the Khalifa; Khilafat Committee (Bombay 1919, Muhammad Ali & Shaukat Ali) — Gandhi linked it to Non-Cooperation to unite Hindus & Muslims.
  • Nagpur session (Dec 1920) adopted Non-Cooperation: surrender titles; boycott civil service, army, police, courts, schools, foreign goods.

🧵 Many Strands, Many "Swaraj"s

  • Towns: foreign cloth boycotted & burnt, imports halved (1921–22) — but khadi was costlier than mill cloth and momentum slowed.
  • Awadh countryside: Baba Ramchandra led peasants against talukdars over high rents & begar (forced labour); Oudh Kisan Sabha (Jawaharlal Nehru).
  • Gudem Hills, AP: Alluri Sitaram Raju led a militant guerrilla struggle — believed force, not non-violence, could free India; executed 1924.
  • Assam plantation workers: swaraj meant freedom to leave and return to their villages, restricted under the Inland Emigration Act, 1859.

CHAURI CHAURA, FEB 1922

  • Violent clash killed policemen → Gandhi called off Non-Cooperation, fearing loss of discipline

🚶 Towards Civil Disobedience

  • C.R. Das & Motilal Nehru form the Swaraj Party; Nehru & Subhas Bose push radical mass action; global depression (from 1926) squeezes peasants.
  • Simon Commission (1928), all-British, met with "Go back Simon" protests.
  • Lahore Congress (Dec 1929) declares "Purna Swaraj"; 26 Jan 1930 observed as Independence Day.
  • Salt March: Gandhi's 11 demands to Viceroy Irwin; walked 240 miles, Sabarmati→Dandi (24 days), broke the salt law 6 Apr 1930 → launched Civil Disobedience — people now broke laws, not just refused cooperation.
  • Gandhi–Irwin Pact (March 1931); movement relaunched 1932, lost steam by 1934.

🌍 How Different Groups Saw the Movement

  • Rich peasants (Patidars, Jats): wanted lower revenue, disappointed the 1931 pact didn't cut rates.
  • Poor peasants: wanted unpaid rent remitted — Congress hesitated, fearing to alienate landlords.
  • Business (FICCI, 1927): backed CDM for tariff protection, but grew wary of prolonged disruption & rising socialism.
  • Industrial workers: limited role (bar Nagpur) — Congress kept their demands sidelined too.
  • Women: huge turnout at the salt march & picketing, yet Gandhi still framed their duty around home & hearth.

🤝 Limits of Civil Disobedience

  • Dr B.R. Ambedkar organised the Depressed Classes Association (1930), sought separate electorates at the 2nd Round Table Conference; Gandhi fasted against this → Poona Pact (Sept 1932) reserved seats for Depressed Classes within the general electorate.
  • Many Muslims felt alienated after Khilafat's decline; Congress seemed closer to the Hindu Mahasabha. Jinnah sought guaranteed representation — talks failed at the 1928 All Parties Conference.

🎇 A Sense of Collective Belonging

  • Bharat Mata — image first created by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay (Vande Mataram, in Anandamath); painted as an ascetic figure by Abanindranath Tagore.
  • Folklore revival to recover a "true" national culture; icons like the 1905 Swadeshi tricolour and Gandhi's 1921 Swaraj flag (spinning wheel at centre).
  • Nationalist writers glorified India's ancient past to counter British claims of backwardness — but this often alienated other communities.
✊

Quit India, 1942: failure of the Cripps Mission + wartime hardship → Congress's "Quit India" resolution (Wardha, 14 July) and Gandhi's "Do or Die" speech (8 Aug, Bombay). A mass movement paralysed parts of the state machinery before being crushed within a year. Congress's long project was, in the end, "a nation with many voices."

📅 Before Gandhi — Setting the Stage

  • The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, spent its early decades as a "moderate" body — petitions, resolutions, and dialogue with the colonial government.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916) saw Congress and the Muslim League agree to work together, briefly uniting Hindus and Muslims against colonial rule ahead of Gandhi's mass movements.
  • Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915, already famous for developing satyagraha against racial laws there.

Pre-modern World · before 1500s

🐫 Silk Routes Link the World

  • Land + sea routes linking Asia ↔ Europe ↔ North Africa, active before the Christian Era, thriving till the 15th century.
  • Carried Chinese silk & pottery, Indian/SE-Asian textiles & spices; gold and silver flowed back from Europe to Asia.
  • Trade meant cultural exchange too — Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers, and Buddhism all spread along these routes.

🥔 Food Travels

  • Noodles likely travelled China → Italy (via Arab traders, 5th-century Sicily) and became spaghetti.
  • Potato, tomato, chilli, maize — unknown to Europe/Asia until Columbus reached the Americas.
  • Europe's new dependence on the potato backfired: 1845–49 Irish Potato Famine killed ~1,000,000.

⚔️ Conquest, Disease & Trade

  • 16th century: sea routes to Asia found, the Atlantic crossed to America — the world "shrank."
  • Spain's deadliest weapon was smallpox — Native Americans had no immunity; whole communities were wiped out ahead of the invaders.
  • By the 18th century, slave-worked plantations in America grew cotton & sugar for Europe.
  • China restricted overseas contact from the 15th century → world trade's centre shifted westward to Europe.
📜

Source to remember: John Winthrop (Massachusetts, 1634) called smallpox a sign of God's blessing on the colonists — an early example of "biological" conquest.

The 19th Century · 1815-1914

🌍 A World Economy Takes Shape

  • Three interlinked flows: trade (goods), labour (migration), capital (investment).
  • Britain scraps the Corn Laws → cheap food imports → British farms can't compete → workers migrate overseas.
  • ~50 million emigrated Europe→America/Australia; ~150 million worldwide left home in the 19th century.
  • World trade grew 25–40× between 1820–1914; 60% was "primary products."

🧊 Role of Technology

  • Railways, steamships & the telegraph reshaped distance and time.
  • Refrigerated ships (from the 1870s) brought cheaper meat to Europe's poor, improving diets.

🏴 Late-19th-century Colonialism

  • 1885 Berlin Conference — European powers carve up Africa with ruler-straight borders.
  • Britain & France expand empires; Belgium, Germany, and even the US (late 1890s) join as colonial powers.

☣️ Rinderpest — the Cattle Plague

  • Arrived via infected cattle imported to feed Italian troops in Eritrea, late 1880s.
  • Spread "like forest fire," killing 90% of Africa's cattle by 1897.
  • Loss of livestock destroyed African livelihoods and forced people into wage labour under colonial control.

🧳 Indentured Labour from India

  • From UP, Bihar, central India, Tamil Nadu → Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji, Ceylon, Malaya, Assam tea gardens.
  • Called a "new system of slavery" — harsh conditions, agents lied about work & travel.
  • Culture survived and fused: Hosay carnival, Rastafarianism, Chutney music. System abolished in 1921.

🏦 Indian Entrepreneurs & Trade

  • Shikaripuri Shroffs & Nattukottai Chettiars financed export agriculture across Central & SE Asia.
  • Indian cotton-textile exports collapsed: 30% (1800) → under 3% (1870s) as British tariffs bit; opium to China became India's largest export from the 1820s.
The Inter-war Economy

⚙️ Wartime Transformations

  • First World War (1914–18): the first fully industrial war — 9 million dead, 20 million injured.
  • Britain borrowed heavily from US banks → the US flips from debtor to creditor nation.

🏭 Mass Production Rises

  • Henry Ford builds the car assembly line in Detroit; Jan 1914 doubles daily wage to $5 to stop workers quitting.
  • US car production: 2 million (1919) → 5 million+ (1929). 1923: US becomes the world's largest overseas lender.

📊 The Great Depression

  • 1929 – mid-1930s: catastrophic fall in production, employment, incomes, trade.
  • Causes: agricultural overproduction + panic withdrawal of US loans + banking collapse — over 4,000 US banks closed by 1933.

🇮🇳 India & the Depression

  • India's exports & imports nearly halved (1928–34); wheat prices fell 50%; Bengal's jute price crashed over 60%.
  • 1931: Gandhi launches the Civil Disobedience Movement amid rural unrest.
Rebuilding a World Economy · post-war

🌐 Bretton Woods System

  • Second World War (1939–45): ~60 million dead, most of them civilians.
  • Jul 1944: Bretton Woods Conference creates the IMF and World Bank — operational from 1947.
  • Fixed exchange rates: currencies pegged to the dollar; dollar pegged to gold at $35/ounce.

🕊️ Decolonisation & the G-77

  • Asian & African colonies win independence but inherit poverty and underdevelopment.
  • Developing nations form the G-77, demanding a New International Economic Order (NIEO).

📈 End of Bretton Woods & the Asian Shift

  • 1970s: dollar-gold link collapses → floating exchange rates begin; developing-world debt crises follow.
  • MNCs relocate production to low-wage Asian economies; China reopens after 1949 to become a manufacturing hub.

🚢 Technology Shrinks the World Further

  • The Suez Canal (1869) cut the sea journey between Europe and Asia dramatically, boosting trade volumes.
  • The telegraph let news and prices travel almost instantly across continents — merchants in London could react to a harvest in Punjab within hours, not months.
  • Faster, cheaper transport made it possible for the same three flows — trade, labour, capital — to move at a scale never seen before 1800.

🏘️ Before the Factory

  • Proto-industrialisation: large-scale production for international markets happened in the countryside, since urban guilds blocked new merchants in towns.
  • Chain: merchant clothier → wool stapler → spinners → weavers → fullers → dyers, with finishing done in London.
  • Open fields vanishing & commons being enclosed pushed peasant families to take up this extra spinning/weaving income.

🧵 Coming of the Factory

  • Earliest factories by the 1730s, multiplying late in the 18th century. Cotton symbolised the new era — raw imports rose from 2.5 million lb (1760) to 22 million lb (1787).
  • Richard Arkwright created the cotton mill, bringing every process under one roof and one management.
  • But industrialisation was slow & uneven: even late in the 19th century under 20% of Britain's workforce worked in technologically advanced sectors; steam engines spread very slowly (only 321 in all England by 1800).

✋ Why Hand Labour Survived

  • Britain had abundant, cheap labour — industrialists were in no rush to install costly machines that displaced human hands (unlike labour-scarce USA).
  • Seasonal industries (gas works, breweries, book-binding, shipbuilding) preferred hand labour for temporary peak demand.
  • Upper classes valued refined, individual handmade goods; machine-made goods were mostly for colonial export.
  • Spinning Jenny (Hargreaves, 1764) was attacked by textile workers who feared unemployment.

🪢 Weavers Under Company Rule

  • Pre-colonial India's silk & fine cotton dominated world textile trade via Surat, Masulipatam, Hoogly, financed by Indian merchant-banker networks.
  • From the 1750s the network broke down as European companies won monopoly trade rights; Bombay & Calcutta rose as colonial ports.
  • Gomasthas (paid Company servants) replaced independent traders, tying weavers exclusively to the Company through advances and often force.

MANCHESTER COMES TO INDIA

  • Indian piece-goods exports fell from 33% (1811-12) to just 3% (1850-51)
  • Weavers hit twice — export markets gone, local markets flooded with cheap Manchester cloth

🏗️ Factories Come Up in India

  • First cotton mill — Bombay, 1854; jute mills — Bengal from 1855; Kanpur, Ahmedabad, Madras mills follow by 1874.
  • Entrepreneurs financed by China trade (opium/tea): Dwarkanath Tagore (Bengal), Parsis (Dinshaw Petit, J.N. Tata), Marwaris (Hukumchand, Birla).
  • Till WWI, European Managing Agencies controlled much industry — Indians supplied capital, Europeans made investment decisions.
  • Workers were controlled at entry by a jobber — a trusted worker who recruited and gained power over new hires.

📈 Peculiarities of Growth

  • Indian mills first made coarse yarn (not cloth) to avoid competing with Manchester; shifted to cloth after 1906 (Swadeshi movement + falling yarn exports to China).
  • WWI cut Manchester imports — Indian mills supplied home needs & war goods (uniforms, tents, jute bags), booming while Britain's textile industry never recovered.
  • Large factories stayed a small slice of the economy — only 5% of the industrial workforce was in registered factories by 1911.
  • Handlooms survived and even expanded — helped by the fly shuttle and by weaving specialised cloth (Banarasi, Baluchari) mills couldn't copy.
🖼️

Selling the goods: "MADE IN MANCHESTER" labels used images of Indian gods and royal figures to build familiarity and trust; calendars carried ads even to non-readers. Indian manufacturers later turned the tables, using the Swadeshi/nationalist message in their own advertising.

🏚️ Life of the Workers

  • Jobs were scarce relative to the number of job-seekers — workers relied on kin & village networks to hear of openings, and could stay unemployed for weeks.
  • Wages depended on the industry and skill, and rose slowly through the 19th century; seasonal unemployment was common, especially after 1918.
  • Some mills gave badli (substitute) workers no job security at all — daily attendance was recorded outside the factory gates each morning.

🀄 The First Printed Books

  • China: earliest print technology from AD 594 — paper rubbed against inked woodblocks; "accordion books"; the imperial state was the biggest producer.
  • Japan: Buddhist missionaries brought hand-printing from China (~AD 768-770); the Diamond Sutra (AD 868) is the oldest printed book. Edo's ukiyo woodblock prints later influenced Manet, Monet, Van Gogh.

🕮 Print Comes to Europe

  • Chinese paper reached Europe via the Silk Route (11th c.); Marco Polo (1295) carried woodblock printing knowledge from China to Italy.
  • Johann Gutenberg (Strasbourg) adapted the olive press & goldsmith's metal moulds to design the first known printing press by 1448; first book: the Bible (~180 copies, 1450s). Moveable type stayed the basic technology for 300 years.
  • 1450–1550: presses spread across Europe — book production boomed from 20 million copies (late 15th c.) to ~200 million (end of 16th c.) — the Print Revolution.

⛪ Print Revolution & Its Impact

  • Cost of books fell, creating a new reading public; oral & print culture blended, since literacy stayed low for a long time.
  • Martin Luther's Ninety Five Theses (1517) printed and spread widely → the Protestant Reformation. Fearing loss of control, the Roman Church issued its Index of Prohibited Books (from 1558).
  • Menocchio, an Italian miller, reinterpreted the Bible from his own reading and was executed by the Inquisition — print could be dangerous.

🧠 The Reading Mania & the 19th Century

  • European literacy rose sharply (60–80% by late 18th c.) → almanacs, chapbooks, periodicals mixing news and entertainment.
  • New readers of the 19th century: children (school textbooks, Grimm tales), women (penny magazines, novelists like Jane Austen, George Eliot), workers (self-improvement tracts, autobiographies).
  • Technology leapt forward: Richard Hoe's power-driven cylindrical press (mid-19th c., 8,000 sheets/hr) → offset press → electric press; selling tricks like serialised novels and cheap paperbacks followed.

🗞️ India and the World of Print

  • A rich manuscript tradition (Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, vernacular) ran alongside print into the late 19th century.
  • Print arrived with Portuguese missionaries at Goa (mid-16th c.) — Konkani, Kanara, Tamil (1579), Malayalam (1713) tracts.
  • James Augustus Hickey's Bengal Gazette (from 1780) was India's first newspaper — gossipy, persecuted by Warren Hastings.

⚖️ Religious Reform & Public Debate

  • Bengal: Rammohun Roy's Sambad Kaumudi vs orthodox Samachar Chandrika, debating widow immolation and priesthood.
  • North India: the ulama used cheap lithographic presses for Persian/Urdu tracts and fatwas (e.g. Deoband Seminary, 1867).
  • Newspapers linked communities across regions, building pan-Indian identities and public opinion — acting like an "opposition" holding government accountable.

📚 New Forms & New Voices

  • Novel, lyric, short story, essay explored personal and social lives; Raja Ravi Varma's mass-produced prints spread new visual culture.
  • Rashsundari Debi's Amar Jiban (1876) — first full-length autobiography in Bengali; Tarabai Shinde & Pandita Ramabai wrote on the miseries of upper-caste Hindu women.
  • Jyotiba Phule's Gulamgiri (1871), Ambedkar, and Periyar used print to challenge caste discrimination.
🚫

Censorship tightened after 1857: the Vernacular Press Act, 1878 (modelled on Irish press laws) allowed seizure of vernacular papers judged seditious. Nationalist papers grew anyway — Tilak's Kesari saw him imprisoned in 1908 — repression only provoked more protest. Around 90 newspapers were suppressed during Quit India (1942).

💡 Print & the French Revolution

  • Print did three things at once: it popularised Enlightenment ideas critiquing tradition and despotism, it created a culture of public debate and questioning, and it spread satire mocking royalty.
  • But print didn't dictate a single opinion — readers met many, even opposing, viewpoints, which opened up new ways of thinking rather than closing them down.
  • England's own printing history began with William Caxton, who set up the country's first press in 1476.
