Chapter 01 · Foundations

Chemical Reactions & Equations

Every rusting nail, ripening fruit, and digested meal is a chemical reaction wearing a disguise. This chapter teaches you how to read the disguise — and write it down properly.

Spotting a Reaction Chemical Equations Types of Reactions Oxidation & Corrosion Glossary Important Reactions Things to Remember Quick Revision Exam Corner
1

How to Spot a Chemical Reaction

Signs that a reaction has happened

A chemical reaction changes the identity of a substance, not just its shape or state. Look for any of these four clues:

  • Change of state — e.g. wax melting is physical, but burning wax to CO₂ + H₂O is chemical.
  • Change of colour — a shiny brown copper vessel turning green.
  • Evolution of a gas — bubbles when zinc meets dilute sulphuric acid.
  • Change of temperature — the flask feels warm/cold as the reaction proceeds.
Amazing factFireworks get their colours from burning metal compounds — strontium salts burn red, copper burns blue-green, and sodium burns the same yellow as a streetlight.
Mg + O₂ → MgO

Magnesium ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame → white magnesium oxide ash.

Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑

Zinc granules + dilute acid → bubbles of hydrogen gas, flask turns warm.

Mistri's tip

Remember the four clues as SCEG — State, Colour, Evolution of gas, temperature Gain/loss. If an exam question describes an "observation," it's testing one of these four.

2

Writing Chemical Equations

Word equation → symbolic equation

A reaction is first written in words: Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide. Reactants sit on the left, products on the right, joined by an arrow that shows the direction of change.

This is shortened to a skeletal (symbolic) equation using chemical formulae — but a skeletal equation isn't automatically correct. It must be balanced.

Balancing by the law of conservation of mass

Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a reaction — so the number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides.

UnbalancedFe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
Balanced3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

Balance by trial and error — adjust coefficients (never subscripts) until atom counts match on both sides.

Making equations more informative

State symbols
(s) solid · (l) liquid · (g) gas · (aq) aqueous (dissolved in water)
Conditions
Written over/under the arrow — Heat, catalyst, pressure, sunlight, electricity
Physical states
Help predict precipitates, e.g. a solid forming in solution = ↓
Fully informative equationCaCO₃(s) --Heat--> CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
3

Types of Chemical Reactions

Amazing factGlow sticks glow through a "cold" chemical reaction (chemiluminescence) — energy is released as light with almost no heat, unlike combustion which releases both.

① Combination reaction

Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat

Quick lime + water → slaked lime (exothermic — releases heat, used in whitewashing)

C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)

② Decomposition reaction — the reverse of combination

A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products. Almost always needs energy input.

Thermal
broken down by heat — CaCO₃ --Δ--> CaO + CO₂ (limestone → quick lime, used in cement industry)
Electrolytic
broken down by electricity — 2H₂O --electricity--> 2H₂ + O₂
Photolytic
broken down by light — 2AgCl --sunlight--> 2Ag + Cl₂ (why silver chloride/bromide is used in black-and-white photography)

③ Displacement reaction

A more reactive element pushes out a less reactive element from its compound.

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Iron nail dipped in copper sulphate solution turns the blue solution pale green, and copper deposits on the nail.

④ Double displacement reaction

Ions are exchanged between two compounds, often producing an insoluble precipitate.

Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)

White precipitate of barium sulphate forms — a classic precipitation reaction.

⑤ Oxidation & Reduction (redox)

Oxidation
gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen
Reduction
loss of oxygen / gain of hydrogen
CuO + H₂ --Δ--> Cu + H₂O

CuO loses oxygen → reduced to Cu. H₂ gains oxygen → oxidised to H₂O. Both happen together — a redox reaction.

Mistri's tip

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss (of hydrogen/electrons), Reduction Is Gain. A displacement reaction is really redox happening between two metals.

Quick-reference: types of reactions

Five reaction types at a glance

🔤 Mnemonic
T · E · P

The three ways to break a compound apart by decomposition: Thermal (heat), Electrolytic (electricity), Photolytic (light). "Tiny Energy Pulses" break the bond.

Common mistake

Students often write Mg + O₂ → MgO₂ — but oxygen is diatomic and magnesium oxide's correct formula is MgO. Always balance with coefficients (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO), never invent new subscripts.

4

Oxidation in Everyday Life

Amazing factRusting silently costs the world's economy billions of dollars every year in damaged bridges, pipelines, ships, and machinery — corrosion is one of the most expensive "slow reactions" on the planet.

Corrosion

A metal is attacked by moisture, acids, or gases around it and slowly eaten away. Silver articles turn black (Ag₂S), copper turns green (basic copper carbonate), and iron rusts (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) — rusting needs both air and water to occur.

Rancidity

Fats and oils in food get oxidised over time, changing smell and taste. Prevented by adding antioxidants, storing food in airtight containers, refrigeration, or packaging in nitrogen gas (as in chip packets).

5

🧠 Things to Remember

Balance firstNever change a subscript to balance an equation — only change the coefficient in front.
Exothermic vs endothermicReactions releasing heat/light are exothermic (e.g. combustion); those absorbing energy are endothermic (e.g. thermal decomposition).
Rust needs two thingsBoth oxygen and moisture must be present — that's why painting/galvanising iron prevents rusting.
Precipitate = insolubleLook for "white/pale precipitate" language — it always signals a double displacement reaction.
Reduction happens at both electrodes' cousinIn a displacement reaction, the more reactive metal is oxidised, the less reactive metal ion is reduced.

Chapter 02 · Reactivity

Acids, Bases & Salts

Sour or soapy, red or blue — this chapter is about how substances behave in water, and why lemon juice, soap, and your stomach all belong to the same story.

Indicators How Acids & Bases React The pH Scale Family of Salts Salts in Daily Life Glossary Important Reactions Things to Remember Quick Revision Exam Corner
1

Acids, Bases & Indicators

An acid turns blue litmus red and tastes sour. A base turns red litmus blue, feels soapy, and — if soluble in water — is called an alkali.

Litmus
purple dye from lichen — pink/red in acid, blue in base
Methyl orange
red in acid, yellow in base
Phenolphthalein
colourless in acid, pink in base
Olfactory indicators
substances whose smell changes with acid/base — e.g. onion loses its odour in a base

Why do acids and bases behave this way?

Acids produce H⁺ ions (as H₃O⁺, hydronium ion) when dissolved in water. Bases produce OH⁻ ions in water. This is the actual reason behind every acid/base property.

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
NaOH(s) --H₂O--> Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Acid vs Base — quick comparison

Side-by-side comparison
PropertyAcidBase
TasteSourBitter
Feel—Soapy
LitmusBlue → RedRed → Blue
Ion released in waterH⁺ (as H₃O⁺)OH⁻
PhenolphthaleinColourlessPink
pH range0–77–14
🔤 Mnemonic
"Blue turns Red, means Acid ahead"

Litmus is naturally blue-ish purple. If it turns red, you're looking at an acid. If red litmus turns back to blue, that's a base at work.

Common mistake

Don't say "acids contain H⁺ ions" as a standalone fact — they release H⁺ ions only when dissolved in water. Dry HCl gas, for instance, does not turn dry litmus paper red.

2

How Acids & Bases React

Acid + Metal

Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑

Test: bring a burning matchstick near the gas — it burns with a pop sound, confirming hydrogen.

Base + (reactive) Metal

2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑

Sodium zincate forms; also releases hydrogen gas.

Acid + Metal carbonate / hydrogencarbonate

Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑
NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑

General rule: Metal carbonate/bicarbonate + Acid → Salt + CO₂ + H₂O. The CO₂ turns lime water milky.

Amazing factYour blood keeps an incredibly narrow pH range of 7.35–7.4 — straying just half a point in either direction can be life-threatening, which is why the body has powerful buffer systems to resist pH change.

Neutralisation — Acid + Base

NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O

Acid + Base → Salt + Water. This is why an antacid (a base) relieves acidity.

Acid/Base + Metal oxide

CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O

Metal oxides are basic — this confirms Metal oxide + Acid → Salt + Water.

3

The pH Scale

pH measures the concentration of H⁺ ions in a solution, on a scale of 0–14.

pH < 7
Acidic — the lower, the more acidic
pH = 7
Neutral (pure water)
pH > 7
Basic — the higher, the more basic

More H⁺ ions = lower pH = stronger acid. Universal indicator shows a whole range of colours to read pH at a glance.

Amazing factThe pH scale is logarithmic — a solution of pH 4 isn't twice as acidic as pH 5, it's ten times more acidic. Dropping just one pH unit means a ten-fold jump in H⁺ concentration.

pH in everyday life

  • Our body works in a narrow pH range (7.0–7.8); tooth decay starts when mouth pH drops below 5.5.
  • Plants & animals are pH-sensitive — acid rain (pH < 5.6) damages aquatic life.
  • Amazing factAcid rain (pH below 5.6) has been slowly dissolving the marble (calcium carbonate) of the Taj Mahal for decades — the same acid-carbonate reaction you studied above, just playing out over centuries on a monument.
  • Digestion needs the stomach's dilute HCl; excess acid causes indigestion, treated with a mild base (antacid).
  • Soil pH must suit the crop — treated with quicklime/slaked lime if too acidic.
4

Family of Salts

A salt is the product of a neutralisation reaction (Acid + Base). Salts with the same positive or negative part belong to the same "family," e.g. NaCl and Na₂SO₄ are both sodium salts.

Strong acid + Strong base
Neutral salt, pH = 7 (e.g. NaCl)
Strong acid + Weak base
Acidic salt, pH < 7 (e.g. NH₄Cl)
Weak acid + Strong base
Basic salt, pH > 7 (e.g. Na₂CO₃)

Water of crystallisation

Fixed number of water molecules chemically bound in one formula unit of a salt — e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue vitriol). Heating removes this water: blue copper sulphate crystals turn white, then turn blue again on adding water.

Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Washing soda — 10 water molecules of crystallisation.

5

Salts You Meet Every Day

Bleaching powder — CaOCl₂

Made by passing chlorine over dry slaked lime. Used for bleaching cotton/linen and wood pulp, as an oxidising agent, and to disinfect drinking water.

Baking soda — NaHCO₃

Sodium hydrogencarbonate; mild, non-corrosive base. Releases CO₂ on heating, which makes cakes rise. Also used in antacids and fire extinguishers.

2NaHCO₃ --Δ--> Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

Washing soda — Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Made by recrystallising sodium carbonate. Used in glass, soap & paper industries, and to remove permanent hardness of water.

Plaster of Paris — CaSO₄·½H₂O

Made by heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at 373 K. Sets into hard gypsum on mixing with water — used for supporting fractured bones and making moulds.

CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O
Mistri's tip

Plaster of Paris must be stored in a moisture-proof container — even air's humidity is enough to slowly set it into gypsum inside the container.

6

🧠 Things to Remember

Never test acids by tasteIn the lab, we test with indicators, not tongue — many acids/bases are corrosive.
Always add acid to waterDissolving acid in water is highly exothermic — add acid to water slowly, never the reverse, or it may splash and burn.
H⁺ never exists aloneIt always attaches to a water molecule as H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion).
More water = weaker, not neutralDiluting an acid/base only reduces ion concentration; pH moves toward 7 but the substance is still acidic/basic.
Gypsum ⇌ Plaster of ParisHeating gypsum removes water to make PoP; adding water to PoP regenerates gypsum. It's a reversible pair.

Chapter 03 · Materials

Metals & Non-metals

Why gold sits untouched for centuries while iron rusts in a season — it all comes down to how badly an element wants to lose or gain electrons.

Physical Properties Chemical Properties Reactivity Series Ionic Compounds Extraction of Metals Corrosion & Alloys Glossary Important Reactions Things to Remember Quick Revision Exam Corner
1

Physical Properties

Amazing factMercury is named after the fast-moving Roman messenger god — fitting, since it's the only metal that stays liquid at room temperature and famously skitters into tiny beads when spilled.

Metals

  • Malleable — can be hammered into thin sheets
  • Ductile — can be drawn into wires
  • Lustrous, and become sonorous when struck
  • Good conductors of heat & electricity
  • Mostly solids at room temperature (Mercury — liquid — is the exception)

Non-metals

  • Neither malleable nor ductile — brittle as solids
  • Poor conductors (except graphite, which conducts)
  • No lustre (except iodine, which is lustrous)
  • Exist as solids, liquids (bromine), or gases
Mistri's tip

Exam favourite exceptions: Mercury (liquid metal), Graphite (conducting non-metal), Iodine (lustrous non-metal), Sodium/Potassium (soft metals, cut with a knife).

2

Chemical Properties

Reaction with oxygen

Metals form basic oxides; non-metals form acidic (or neutral) oxides.

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Al₂O₃ is amphoteric — reacts with both acids and bases.

Metals like Na and K react so vigorously with oxygen that they're stored under kerosene oil. Metals like gold and silver do not react with oxygen at all, even at high temperature.

Reaction with water

Reactivity varies hugely: K and Na react so violently they can catch fire; Ca reacts calmly (floats due to bubbles sticking to it); Mg reacts with hot water/steam only; Fe reacts only with steam; Pb, Cu, Ag, Au do not react with water at all.

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + heat
3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

Reaction with dilute acids

Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + Hydrogen

Rate of bubble formation shows reactivity: Mg > Al > Zn > Fe is fastest to slowest. Copper does not react with dilute HCl at all. (Note: HNO₃ usually doesn't give H₂ gas — it's a strong oxidiser — except with very dilute HNO₃ + Mg or Mn.)

Reaction with other metal salt solutions

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.

3

The Reactivity Series

Amazing factAluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust — more common than iron — but it was once pricier than gold, because early extraction methods couldn't overcome its extreme reactivity. Napoleon III reportedly served state guests on aluminium plates as a status symbol.

Metals arranged in decreasing order of reactivity — this single list predicts almost every reaction in this chapter.

KNaCaMgAlZnFePb[H]CuAgAu

Most reactive on the left, least reactive (most "noble") on the right. A metal higher up can displace any metal below it from a salt solution.

🔤 Mnemonic
"Please Send Cats, Monkeys And Zebras In For Cooking Help, Cooking Snacks After Games"

K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, [H], Cu, Ag, Au — a silly sentence is often the fastest way to lock in the reactivity series order for exams.

Common mistake

Don't assume every metal reacts with water or dilute acid — copper, silver and gold do neither. Reactivity, not "being a metal," decides the outcome.

4

How Ionic Compounds Form

Metals lose electrons from their outer shell to form positive ions (cations); non-metals gain those electrons to form negative ions (anions). Oppositely charged ions attract and form an ionic (electrovalent) bond.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl, held together by strong electrostatic force.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points — strong forces between ions
  • Generally soluble in water, insoluble in kerosene/petrol
  • Conduct electricity only when molten or in solution (ions become free to move); do not conduct as solids
5

Extraction of Metals

The extraction method depends entirely on where a metal sits in the reactivity series.

Top (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al)
Highly reactive — extracted by electrolytic reduction of molten ores/oxides
Middle (Fe, Zn, Pb, Cu)
Moderately reactive — extracted by reducing the oxide with carbon (in a blast furnace, roasting/calcination first)
Bottom (Hg, Ag, Au)
Least reactive — found as free elements, or extracted just by heating their compounds

Key steps for the middle group

  1. Enrichment of the ore to remove impurities (gangue)
  2. Calcination (heating carbonate ore, limited air) or Roasting (heating sulphide ore, excess air) to convert to oxide
  3. Reduction of the metal oxide, e.g. with carbon
  4. Refining — commonly by electrolytic refining for purity
ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)

The Thermit reaction

Fe₂O₃(s) + 2Al(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s) + Heat

Highly exothermic — molten iron produced is used to join railway tracks/cracked machine parts.

6

Corrosion & Alloys

Amazing factHumans have been alloying metals since around 3300 BCE — mixing copper with tin to make bronze gave the Bronze Age its name. Stainless steel, by contrast, is a 20th-century invention, first developed by Harry Brearley in 1913 while he was trying to make gun barrels resist erosion.

Corrosion

Metal surface attacked by moisture, acids, or gases in air. Iron rusting is the most common example (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). Prevented by painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (zinc coating), or alloying.

Alloys

A homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or a metal and non-metal) that improves properties — e.g. stainless steel (Fe+Cr+Ni, doesn't rust), brass (Cu+Zn), bronze (Cu+Sn), solder (Pb+Sn, low melting point).

📖 Glossary

Malleability
Ability of a metal to be hammered into thin sheets.
Ductility
Ability of a metal to be drawn into thin wires.
Ionic (electrovalent) bond
A bond formed by the transfer of electrons and attraction between resulting ions.
Ore
A mineral from which a metal can be profitably extracted.
Gangue
Unwanted rocky/earthy impurities mixed with an ore.
Calcination
Heating a carbonate ore strongly in limited air to convert it into an oxide.
Roasting
Heating a sulphide ore strongly in excess air to convert it into an oxide.
Amphoteric oxide
A metal oxide that reacts with both acids and bases, e.g. Al₂O₃, ZnO.
Alloy
A homogeneous mixture of a metal with another metal or non-metal, made to improve its properties.
Noble metal
A metal (e.g. gold, platinum) that resists corrosion and oxidation, sitting at the bottom of the reactivity series.
Sonorous
Producing a ringing sound when struck — a property of metals.
Refining
Final purification step of an extracted metal, often by electrolysis.
Thermit reaction
Highly exothermic reaction of aluminium with a metal oxide (e.g. Fe₂O₃), used in welding.

🧠 Things to Remember

Reactivity decides everythingExtraction method, displacement outcome, and even storage (K/Na under kerosene) all trace back to the reactivity series.
Ionic compounds don't conduct as solidsIons are locked in a rigid lattice — only free to move (and conduct) once molten or dissolved.

Galvanising ≠ paintingGalvanising coats iron with a reactive layer of zinc that corrodes first, sacrificially protecting the iron underneath.

Why is sodium stored under kerosene?
It's so reactive it catches fire on contact with air/moisture — kerosene keeps both away.
Why can't we use displacement to extract sodium?
Sodium is too reactive — no cheaper metal can displace it, so electrolytic reduction is used instead.
Why do ionic compounds have high melting points?
Strong electrostatic forces of attraction bind the oppositely charged ions tightly in a lattice.
What makes stainless steel special?
Iron alloyed with chromium and nickel — hard, doesn't rust or corrode.

Carbon & Its Compounds

Carbon makes up barely 0.02% of the earth's crust — yet it forms more compounds than every other element combined. This is the chapter that explains why.

Covalent Bonding Carbon's Versatility Homologous Series Chemical Properties Ethanol & Ethanoic Acid Soaps & Detergents Glossary Important Reactions Things to Remember Quick Revision Exam Corner
1

Why Carbon Bonds Covalently

Amazing factAbout 18% of the human body's mass is carbon — the second most abundant element in you after oxygen. Every protein, fat, and strand of DNA in your body is a carbon compound.

Carbon has 4 electrons in its outermost shell. It could gain 4 electrons (unstable — too much negative charge to hold), or lose 4 electrons (needs huge energy) — so instead it shares electrons with other atoms.

This sharing forms a covalent bond — single (1 shared pair), double (2 pairs), or triple (3 pairs).

Properties of covalent compounds

  • Generally low melting and boiling points (weak intermolecular forces)
  • Usually poor conductors of electricity — no free ions or electrons
  • Often insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents
2

Carbon's Versatile Nature

Catenation

Carbon's unique ability to form bonds with other carbon atoms, creating long chains, branched chains, or rings. No other element does this as extensively — this alone explains the huge variety of carbon compounds.

Tetravalency

Carbon forms 4 bonds, allowing it to bond with other carbon atoms and with atoms of oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulphur, chlorine, and more — giving compounds with very different properties.

Amazing factDiamond and graphite are made of exactly the same element — carbon — arranged differently. Diamond's rigid 3D lattice makes it the hardest known natural substance, while graphite's loosely stacked sheets make it soft enough to write with.

Saturated vs unsaturated hydrocarbons

Alkanes (CnH2n+2)
Saturated — only single bonds, e.g. CH₄ (methane), C₂H₆ (ethane)
Alkenes (CnH2n)
Unsaturated — one double bond, e.g. C₂H₄ (ethene)
Alkynes (CnH2n-2)
Unsaturated — one triple bond, e.g. C₂H₂ (ethyne)
3

A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group and general formula, where each member differs from the next by a –CH₂ unit (14 u in molecular mass). Members show a gradual change in physical properties but similar chemical properties.

🔤 Mnemonic
Mad Elephants Prefer Bananas

Meth(1), Eth(2), Prop(3), But(4) — the first four carbon-count prefixes, in order, that name almost every compound in this chapter.

Common mistake

Don't confuse "saturated" with "stable" — saturated just means only single bonds. Unsaturated compounds (alkenes/alkynes) are more reactive because their double/triple bonds can break for addition reactions.

Mistri's tip

Naming a carbon compound = (count the carbons for the base word: meth-1, eth-2, prop-3, but-4) + (suffix for the functional group: -ane/-ene/-yne, -ol, -al, -oic acid).

4

Chemical Properties of Carbon Compounds

Amazing factLPG cylinders are naturally odourless — a foul-smelling sulphur compound (ethyl mercaptan) is deliberately added so that even a tiny gas leak can be smelled and caught before it becomes dangerous.

① Combustion

Carbon compounds burn in oxygen to give CO₂, water, heat and light — this is why they're used as fuels. Saturated hydrocarbons usually burn with a clean blue flame; unsaturated/aromatic ones burn with a yellow, sooty flame.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat & light

② Oxidation

Alcohols can be oxidised to carboxylic acids using an oxidising agent like alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇.

CH₃CH₂OH --[O]--> CH₃COOH

③ Addition reaction

Unsaturated hydrocarbons add hydrogen in the presence of a catalyst (Ni/Pd) to become saturated — this is hydrogenation, used to convert vegetable oils into vegetable ghee (vanaspati).

④ Substitution reaction

Saturated hydrocarbons are fairly unreactive but react with chlorine in the presence of sunlight — one hydrogen atom at a time gets substituted by chlorine.

CH₄ + Cl₂ --sunlight--> CH₃Cl + HCl
5

Ethanol & Ethanoic Acid — Two Key Compounds

Ethanol (C₂H₅OH)

  • Good solvent; also present in medicines like tincture iodine, cough syrups, and tonics
  • Reacts with sodium, liberating hydrogen gas
  • Long-term consumption damages the liver; a small amount is used as a beverage but consumption of denatured/impure alcohol can be fatal
2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂↑
CH₃CH₂OH --hot conc. H₂SO₄--> CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O

Dehydration of ethanol — conc. sulphuric acid acts as a dehydrating agent, removing water.

Ethanoic acid / acetic acid (CH₃COOH)

Commonly called acetic acid; 5–8% solution in water is called vinegar. Melting point 290 K, so it often freezes in cold climates — hence also called glacial acetic acid.

NaOH + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑

Unlike mineral acids, weak carboxylic acids like this still react with carbonates to release CO₂.

CH₃COOH + Ethanol --acid catalyst--> Ester + H₂O

Esterification — produces a sweet-smelling ester, used in perfumes and as flavouring agents.

6

Soaps & Detergents

Saponification

Soap = sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid, made by heating an ester (fat/oil) with NaOH — the ester breaks back down into alcohol and the sodium salt (soap). Since this reaction is used to make soap, it's called saponification.

How soap cleans — the micelle

A soap molecule has two ends with different properties: a hydrophilic ionic head (attracted to water) and a hydrophobic carbon-chain tail (attracted to oil/dirt). In water, soap molecules cluster with tails pointing inward around a grease/dirt droplet, forming a micelle — this traps dirt and lets it be rinsed away.

Soap vs detergent in hard water

Hard water contains calcium/magnesium salts that react with soap to form an insoluble scum, wasting soap and leaving marks on clothes. Detergents are ammonium or sulphonate salts of long-chain acids that don't form this scum, so they work even in hard water.

🧠 Things to Remember

Catenation + tetravalencyTogether, these two properties explain why carbon alone forms millions of compounds.
–CH₂ = 14uThat's the mass jump between consecutive members of any homologous series — a fast way to identify the series.
Vinegar is dilute, glacial is pureVinegar = 5–8% ethanoic acid in water. Glacial acetic acid is the pure, undiluted form that freezes in winter.
Soap fails, detergent doesn'tHard water's Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions react with soap to form scum — detergents avoid this problem entirely.
Micelle, not dissolvingSoap doesn't chemically dissolve oil — it physically traps it inside a micelle so water can carry it away.

Why does carbon form covalent, not ionic, bonds?
Losing or gaining 4 electrons both need too much energy — sharing electrons is far more stable.
What turns vegetable oil into vanaspati ghee?
Hydrogenation — an addition reaction adding H₂ across double bonds using a Ni catalyst.

Pure ethanoic acid freezes into ice-like crystals in cold weather (m.p. 290 K).
What makes an ester smell sweet?
Esters formed from acids + alcohols are typically fragrant — used in perfumes and flavourings.
